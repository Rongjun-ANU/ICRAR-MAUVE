## A. Revised scientific argument and literature assessment

### A1. The central argument after the expanded search

The paper should ask: **How are the intensity and spatial coverage of star formation connected to the distribution and excitation of detectable ionised gas across the adopted Virgo infall-stage populations?** This question accommodates the full sequence of proposed results: the rSFMS comparison; the decline in conditional SF intensity; the limited cases of local excess; the changing SF/NSF/ND census; and the weaker but differently excited emission outside the adopted SF selection.

The literature now supports a more specific starting point than yesterday's Introduction. Environmental studies have already established reduced SF, truncated discs, changes in the luminosities of H II regions, reduced molecular-gas content and efficiency, extended low-ionisation emission, and links between stripping state and excitation. The opportunity for Paper II is to measure how these manifestations are connected **within one spatially resolved census**, retaining the distinction between the fraction of the usable footprint forming stars and the intensity of SF within that selected component.

The proposed working title is:

> **MAUVE-MUSE: star formation suppression and changing ionised-gas excitation along the Virgo infall sequence**

Here, 'sequence' refers to the adopted pre-peak, close-to-peak, and post-peak populations. The text must explain that these are different galaxies, rather than repeated observations of an individual galaxy or nebula. A more conservative title, if the stage calibration remains a substantial uncertainty, is **MAUVE-MUSE: the spatial connection between star formation suppression and ionised-gas excitation in Virgo galaxies**. Neither title requires 'residual emission', a universal density decline, or a uniquely established HOLMES contribution.

### A2. What the expanded search covers

The search followed six connected questions: the global environmental dependence of SF; the spatial suppression of SF in Virgo and other clusters; the luminosity and surface brightness of the remaining SF component; the distinction between molecular-gas supply and SF efficiency; the spatial census of weak/non-H II-like emission; and competing explanations for its excitation. Search terms combined the survey names with 'H II luminosity', 'surface brightness', 'resolved main sequence', 'truncation', 'quenching', 'diffuse ionised gas', 'LIER', 'equivalent width', 'gas density', and 'star formation efficiency'. Reference lists were followed from the closest studies to earlier precedents and newer results.

The enlarged coverage includes foundational environmental work; VESTIGE imaging and individual-region studies through 2026; VIVA histories; VERTICO I-VII; GASP resolved SF, clump, DIG, quenching-history, and full-sample studies; MaNGA and SAMI environmental and excitation analyses; PHANGS-MUSE and MAD comparisons; and relevant ionisation models. The bibliography also retains selected discussion references that do not all belong in the Introduction. Survey publication pages were discovery tools: conclusions below are attached to individual papers, because a survey name does not specify a selection, tracer, or estimator.

This is a comprehensive targeted narrative review, rather than a formally registered systematic review or a demonstration of absolute bibliographic completeness. In particular, a negative search result cannot establish that an observational combination has never been published. The appropriate contribution statement is therefore a defined extension of the nearest precedents, without an unrestricted 'first' claim.

### A3. The measurements that must not be conflated

The missing distinction in the previous draft was not merely between detected and undetected SF. Several different observables are described informally as 'fading', and they answer different questions.

| Observable | What it measures | Relevance to MAUVE |
|---|---|---|
| Number of segmented H II regions above a luminosity limit | The abundance of identified nebulae, with a catalogue completeness boundary | Relevant to the loss of SF sites, but not identical to the area fraction of SF spaxels |
| Integrated Halpha luminosity of one region | Its total recombination luminosity; depends on size, stellar content, age, attenuation, leakage, and blending | A lower value does not automatically mean lower SFR per unit area |
| Brightest-region luminosity or a luminosity function | The bright extreme or distribution of a regional population | Depends on the number of sampled regions and on the host quantity held fixed |
| Halpha luminosity per projected region area | Surface brightness of a segmented nebula | Closer to intensity, but the segmentation and PSF remain relevant |
| SFR surface density in SF-selected spaxels at fixed stellar surface density | The strength of recent SF conditional on passing the adopted selection | The most direct description of the stage-separated MAUVE rSFMS |
| SF area fraction | The fraction of a specified usable footprint assigned to SF | Describes coverage, rather than the brightness of the selected component |
| Molecular SF efficiency, SFR/molecular-gas mass | The rate of forming stars per unit molecular reservoir | Requires gas information; a suppressed rSFMS alone does not measure it |
| DIG Halpha flux fraction | The diffuse contribution to the total Halpha luminosity under a particular decomposition | Can increase while the diffuse luminosity decreases; is not an NSF area fraction |

For a consistently weighted footprint, its mean SF-attributed SFR surface density can be expressed as the SF coverage multiplied by the conditional mean SF intensity. With equal-galaxy averaging, the conditional intensity associated with this identity must use the corresponding occupancy weights; it is not generally the arithmetic mean of the per-galaxy conditional intensities. This decomposition gives Section 4 a physical purpose: it distinguishes a smaller SF footprint from weaker SF within that footprint. It should not be relabelled as a catalogue of individual nebular luminosities.

The manuscript-safe description of the present result is therefore **'lower SFR surface density among SF-selected spaxels at comparable local stellar surface density in the later-stage populations'**. 'Fainter surviving H II regions' is acceptable shorthand only after explaining the observational meaning. 'Individual H II regions fade along infall' would require a temporal inference that the current population comparison does not directly provide.

### A4. Do previous studies find that H II regions are fainter, as well as fewer?

**Yes, there is directly relevant precedent, but it does not support a universal fading of every surviving region.** The earlier report cited the regional-abundance result while omitting the more direct luminosity literature. [Boselli et al. (2020; VESTIGE VI)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201937310) establishes the deficit of outer H II regions on approximately 50 pc scales. It should not be used alone to substantiate a decline in conditional SF intensity. [Boselli et al. (2025; VESTIGE XVII)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202450963) supplies the regional luminosity, size, and density baseline in H I-normal systems.

The crucial omitted comparison is [Boselli et al. (2026; VESTIGE XXI)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202659463): the brightest/first-three-ranked regions are less luminous at fixed host stellar properties, but have similar luminosities at fixed SFR. Perturbed systems have a steeper composite faint end and brighter characteristic luminosity, without a clear trend in individual-galaxy fit parameters. They also show more high-density regions and a larger diffuse Halpha contribution. This is not uniform nebular dimming.

This result materially changes the priority assessment. Paper II cannot claim that environmentally affected galaxies were previously known only to contain fewer regions, with the intensity of the remaining SF entirely unexplored. Equally, it should not claim a one-to-one confirmation of its own rSFMS result: a host-mass-conditioned brightest-region statistic differs from a local-stellar-density-conditioned spaxel statistic. An extreme regional luminosity can respond to the number of draws from the luminosity distribution. Testing an intrinsic shift of the regional population therefore requires completeness and sampling controls, rather than only a comparison of the brightest object.

There are also clear precedents for retained or enhanced intensity. [Koopmann & Kenney (2004a,b)](https://doi.org/10.1086/423190) established that truncated Virgo discs can retain approximately normal or enhanced inner SF, alongside other morphological classes ([companion morphology study](https://doi.org/10.1086/423191)). [Boselli et al. (2022; VESTIGE XII)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202142482) finds compact star-forming discs in Virgo lenticulars with SF surface brightness comparable to late-type galaxies, despite low integrated SFRs associated with their small extent. This is an especially useful demonstration that a galaxy can move below the global SFMS without every surviving SF location becoming faint.

For enhancement on individual-region scales, [Boselli et al. (2021; VESTIGE IX)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202039046) connects bright, denser H II regions in IC 3476 to gas compression. In six strongly stripped GASP galaxies, [Giunchi et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/acc5ee) finds Halpha clumps brighter at a given size than comparison regions in ordinary local star-forming galaxies. That sample is a relevant counterexample to universal suppression, not evidence that all infalling galaxies should enhance their SF. H II luminosity distributions also vary with internal galactic environment and ionisation properties in PHANGS-MUSE ([Santoro et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141907)).

The unresolved question that MAUVE can address is consequently narrower and more useful: **after using the same SF selection and a common range of local stellar surface density, does the conditional intensity decline across the adopted stages, and how much of the overall decline is associated with reduced SF coverage?** A measured decline in both quantities would establish simultaneous changes in the abundance and strength of SF-selected emission. It would connect the earlier regional, radial, and gas-scaling results without claiming that every nebula undergoes the same evolution.

### A5. VERTICO: suppressed SF is not synonymous with exhausted molecular gas

The VERTICO sequence belongs near the centre of the literature argument. Its relevance extends beyond a single citation to the rSFMS. The survey design is described by [Brown et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/ac28f5), and the following studies separate several routes to reduced SF.

| Study | Result needed for Paper II | Consequence for the interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| [Zabel et al. (2022), VERTICO II](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac6e68) | H I-deficient systems have steeper, truncated molecular-gas profiles, especially through loss of low-surface-density outer material | A model retaining all molecular gas is a restricted scenario |
| [Jimenez-Donaire et al. (2023), VERTICO III](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202244718) | An approximately linear ensemble KS relation coexists with galaxy-to-galaxy differences and lower molecular efficiency in H I-deficient systems | A familiar ensemble relation does not imply environmental invariance |
| [Villanueva et al. (2022), VERTICO IV](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac9d3c) | Within the effective radius, increasingly perturbed H I is accompanied by lower molecular SF efficiency | Inner suppression can occur while a substantial molecular reservoir persists |
| [Watts et al. (2023), VERTICO V](https://doi.org/10.1017/pasa.2023.14) | Environmental effects lower the surface density of retained atomic gas and, in strongly deficient galaxies, molecular gas | The retained inner ISM is not necessarily unaffected |
| [Roberts et al. (2023), VERTICO VI](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202346599) | Leading halves of H I-tailed discs show excess molecular gas and SF, with an approximately unchanged depletion time | Local enhancement can reflect more fuel without enhanced efficiency |
| [Brown et al. (2023), VERTICO VII](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/acf195) | At 720 pc, resolved SF suppression is associated with both reduced molecular content and reduced efficiency | This is a close precedent for weaker SF at fixed local stellar density |

These papers make two aspects of the proposed MAUVE interpretation more compelling. First, the remaining inner ionised emission should be considered in the context of a transformed, potentially depleted or inefficient cold-gas reservoir. Second, the NGC 4654 excess should be framed as a local departure within a population that can be suppressed overall. Neither interpretation requires the gas content or the efficiency to respond in the same way everywhere. Earlier Virgo measurements already found that enhanced molecular efficiency is not a universal response to ram pressure ([Vollmer et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201118690)).

### A6. GASP, MaNGA, and other resolved precedents

**GASP establishes both enhancement and later suppression.** The integrated enhancement study of [Vulcani et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/aae68b), the resolved SFR-mass analysis of [Vulcani et al. (2020b)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aba4ae), and the HST clump study cited above belong together. They describe selected stripping populations, with different physical scales and tracers. They do not require a population-wide enhancement in a sample extending to strongly suppressed stages. The abruptly quenched systems studied by [Vulcani et al. (2020a)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ab7bdd) and the full MUSE sample of [Poggianti et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202554200) supply the necessary late-stage context. The latter also discusses selection incompleteness and galaxies with centrally restricted gas discs.

The wider literature does not show a universal enhancement. [Foster et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13123) finds no systematic asymmetric SF enhancement in a large UNIONS satellite sample, while [Goller et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09199) finds no population-wide enhancement in simulated TNG50 jellyfish despite bursts along individual histories. [Moretti et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202558175) reports normal-to-low efficiencies in four GASP jellyfish, with substantial dependence on location and gas density. These comparisons favour a stage-, selection-, and scale-dependent account rather than a single expected sign of the environmental response.

**The GASP DIG papers directly constrain the proposed novelty.** [Tomicic et al. (2021a)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abca93) compares stripped and control galaxies and finds no clear difference in their inferred DIG flux fractions. A larger MAUVE NSF area fraction would not contradict that result, because its denominator and classification differ. [Tomicic et al. (2021b)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac230e) links enhanced [O I]/Halpha in diffuse tails to additional ionisation processes. [Tomicic et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ad7130) associates UV/Halpha discrepancies in DIG-dominated regions of late-stage systems with recently declining SF. That study also identifies Halpha-emitting areas without a UV counterpart and with non-SF excitation. Thus, neither detectable gas after declining SF nor a changing relation between SF tracers is unprecedented.

There is also a particularly close conceptual antecedent outside GASP. [Pedrini et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stac345) relates interface-region excitation, SF activity, and gas-disc truncation to different stripping phases in A1367 and ESO 137-001. Its late-stage interpretation emphasises low SF and shocks at the interface. This is not the same geometry or estimator as MAUVE's inner-disc NSF census, but it rules out presenting the general connection between stripping stage and excitation as new. The relevant sections of that paper and [Fossati et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz136) support mixed photoionisation and shocks; they should not be cited alone as proof of old-star ionisation in MAUVE discs.

**MaNGA provides both environmental SF and weak-emission precedents.** [Spindler et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/sty247) finds lower satellite specific SFRs across radii; [Lian et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz2218) finds an environmental dependence of SF profiles particularly in low-mass satellites; and [Bluck et al. (2020a,b)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz3264) separates the predictors of regional SF from quenching and examines the radial patterns of satellite quenching ([radial analysis](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staa2806)). Some of these analyses supplement emission-line SFRs with continuum-based estimates in weak-line regions. Their 'quenched spaxels' therefore cannot be equated with MAUVE's Balmer non-detections or NSF selection.

[Belfiore et al. (2016, 2017)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw1234) already establishes extended low-ionisation emission, weak equivalent widths, and the resolved transition towards quiescence ([transition study](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3211)). [Zhang et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3308) establishes the connection between low Halpha surface brightness and enhanced forbidden-line ratios. Central LIER emission in a galaxy that forms stars further out is not, by itself, an outside-in stripping sequence; the environmental context must be supplied independently.

The SAMI results strengthen the comparison. [Schaefer et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw2289) and [Medling et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/sty127) provide environmental and resolved-SF precedents. More directly, [Cakir et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1017/pasa.2026.10157) compares unperturbed, asymmetric, and truncated cluster galaxies, finding stronger outer suppression in the more affected classes without a corresponding central enhancement. This is a close contemporary comparison for the proposed stage-dependent rSFMS, although at a coarser physical resolution.

### A7. What can be claimed, and what the nearest precedents already establish

| MAUVE finding or intended claim | Established context | Defensible addition in Paper II |
|---|---|---|
| The full resolved SF distribution overlaps a nearby-disc comparison | CALIFA, MAD, PHANGS, and GASP establish resolved SF relations with selection-dependent scatter | Test the comparison with explicitly aligned scale, footprint, and treatment of non-detections |
| Later stages have weaker conditional SF intensity | VESTIGE regional luminosities, VERTICO resolved suppression, and SAMI/MaNGA environmental profiles already bear on this question | Resolve the stage dependence at MAUVE scales and quantify it separately from coverage |
| Outer SF coverage declines | Virgo Halpha imaging, VESTIGE, stellar-population studies, and GASP already establish truncation and outside-in suppression | Measure the accompanying SF/NSF/ND redistribution over the same usable footprint |
| Inner regions retain emission outside the SF selection | MaNGA LIERs, VESTIGE inner gas structures, GASP DIG studies, and prior MAUVE work provide precedents | Establish where the NSF area fraction changes with stage and local stellar density |
| NSF emission becomes fainter while diagnostic ratios rise | DIG surface-brightness sequences and environmental excitation differences are established | Compare absolute line surface brightness and ratios together within the stage-selected NSF population |
| Local enhancement can coexist with broad suppression | IC 3476, GASP clumps, VERTICO asymmetries, and NGC 4654 studies already show local responses | Quantify the NGC 4654 angular excess relative to a fixed internal SF reference |
| A declining young-star contribution reveals harder emission | HOLMES, leakage models, and Brown et al. (2026) already develop relevant interpretations | Test a restricted supply-decline model against the jointly measured spatial and spectroscopic trends |

The closest MAUVE precedent must be explicit. [Brown et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae632e) already argues that suppressed SF exposes a larger DIG contribution, with a secondary shock-like component, in an early MAUVE sample. Paper II should build on that interpretation by resolving the stage and spatial dependence of the census, conditional SF intensity, and NSF line brightness. 'Environmental suppression unveils DIG' is therefore background to the proposed paper, rather than its standalone new conclusion.

For the PHANGS comparison, similar nominal spatial resolution does not establish equivalent measurements. The present notebook uses native spatial products rather than a demonstrated common-PSF 100 pc reconstruction; its upper SFR proxy includes NSF line emission and non-detection limits. This differs from the linear averaging and DIG treatment in [Pessa et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202140733). PHANGS also includes Virgo members and is not a strictly independent field-only control. The correct provisional statement is broad overlap under the stated comparison, with quantitative equivalence requiring matched estimators. MAD and VESTIGE additionally show that PHANGS is not the only relevant high-resolution survey, although it is an especially useful MUSE comparison.

### A8. Consequences for the physical discussion

The outside-in pattern remains a strong organising observation, but its physical interpretation should use the gas morphology, stage information, and stellar constraints together. [Morgan et al. (2024; VESTIGE XVI)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202449225) shows that a starvation model incorporating a critical gas surface density can produce moderate SF-disc truncation. It discusses early starvation followed by stronger ram pressure. Accordingly, 'truncation excludes starvation' is too broad; the strength, timescale, and accompanying gas signatures determine the comparison. The more detailed NGC 4330 reconstruction of [Fossati et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201732373) illustrates how stellar information can constrain a rapid, radially structured decline.

The 20 October supply-decline model is useful as a controlled explanation, but it is not a complete environmental model. It suppresses replenishment while retaining a simplified molecular reservoir and prescribed conversion/depletion rates. VERTICO and [Boselli et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201322313) motivate alternatives involving direct molecular-gas loss or changing efficiency. Morgan et al. also supplies a close precedent for coupled atomic and molecular reservoirs during environmental quenching. Paper II should state which additional connection is made to the ionising-source mixture, rather than presenting the reservoir structure itself as unprecedented.

A declining line surface brightness and a rising line ratio need not imply an increase in the absolute power supplied by a hard source. They can arise when the components decline at different rates. Hot low-mass evolved stars (HOLMES), including post-AGB stars, are one candidate distributed source ([Flores-Fajardo et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2011.18848.x); [Byler et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ab1b70)). Leaked stellar radiation is another: [Belfiore et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141859) and the October preprint of [Baron et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07163) give distinct, relevant treatments of diffuse excitation. The latter particularly cautions against assigning all hard, faint emission in star-forming discs to old stars.

In the model, a persistent ionising-photon production rate is not a fixed nebular luminosity floor: the gas must still intercept and absorb those photons. Conversely, a non-detection is a sensitivity statement, not a direct measure of zero gas mass. These points are essential when connecting a fading SF component to detectable NSF and undetected Balmer emission.

The density and dispersion diagnostics remain secondary tests. H II-region density estimates in the VESTIGE studies concern a different gas selection and estimator from an NSF [S II] doublet measurement. The current MAUVE saved profiles do not establish a universal increase of [S II]6716/6731 across stages, and the low-density limit limits its resolving power. Neither a universal density decline nor monotonic dynamical heating should be written as a result before those analyses are checked. [Law et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abfe0a), [Kewley et al. (2019a)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ab16ed), and [Barnes et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab2958) supply the appropriate diagnostic context.

Finally, the local enhancement case needs its existing history. [Lizee et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202038910) already identifies compressed gas and enhanced efficiency in NGC 4654, while [Vollmer (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361:20021729) models combined gravitational and ram-pressure effects. The proposed Fourier result adds a quantified angular association; it does not establish RPS as the unique cause of the asymmetry. NGC 4424's merger, central activity, and outflow interpretation have a direct reference in [Boselli et al. (2018b)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201833914). Other MAUVE outflow studies further motivate distinguishing feedback from an environmental excitation signature.

### A9. Terminology: should the paper use 'residual emission'?

**The phrase is grammatically natural, but it is not the best primary classification for this paper.** In the relevant literature, DIG, LIER emission, non-H II-like excitation, and emission associated with weak recent SF have more specific meanings. The targeted search did not establish 'residual emission' as a standard umbrella term for all of these. This does not mean the phrase is prohibited or never used.

| Term | Recommended use |
|---|---|
| NSF-selected emission | Preferred label for the actual complement of the adopted SF selection, with the detection and quality requirements stated |
| Emission outside the adopted SF selection | Clearest wording at first use and in discussion of the census |
| Non-H II-like emission/excitation | Use when valid line-ratio classifications support this distinction; not automatically for every NSF-selected spaxel |
| LIER-like emission | Use for the low-ionisation subset supported by the diagnostic diagrams; not a synonym for all non-SF emission or proof of an AGN |
| Diffuse ionised gas, or DIG | Use when a spatial or emission-decomposition criterion identifies a diffuse component; DIG can be photoionised by young stars |
| Weak line emission | Describes intensity only; it does not identify the source or geometry |
| Residual ionised emission | Possible descriptive wording after defining what remains detectable, but avoid as a category name or an assumed evolutionary remnant |

The main semantic problem with 'residual' is that it can suggest a subtraction residual or a remnant of an earlier phase. The data measure emission in a selected population; they do not track the same gas through time. 'Remnant H II emission' would be more problematic because it could suggest a recombining nebula persisting without a continuing energy source. A model-specific residual should be named explicitly, for example 'the emission remaining after subtraction of the modelled H II-region contribution'.

A suitable first-use definition is:

> We divide the usable footprint into SF-selected regions, regions without the required Balmer detections, and detected regions outside the adopted SF selection. We refer to the last category as NSF-selected emission. This observational category does not assume a unique ionising source or a purely diffuse gas morphology.

Subsequently, the paper can describe a subset as 'predominantly LIER-like' or 'consistent with a substantial DIG contribution' if the measurements support that wording. The abbreviation NSF need not assert that the true SFR is exactly zero. This distinction is especially relevant because the current selection also uses equivalent width and gas dispersion, rather than a single excitation boundary.

### A10. The resulting manuscript progression

Section 3 establishes the behaviour of the SF-selected component: the nearby-survey comparison, stage-dependent conditional intensity, and the NGC 4654 local excess. Section 4 then asks how much of the observed disc retains such emission, and where lower SF coverage is accompanied by NSF-selected emission or Balmer non-detection. Section 5 measures the absolute brightness and excitation of the detected NSF component. Section 6 tests whether declining SF and a changing mixture of ionising sources can explain these connected observations, while considering gas depletion, reduced efficiency, leakage, shocks, and feedback.

The main result can be framed without an unsupported priority claim:

> We connect the stage dependence of the intensity of SF-selected emission to changes in its spatial coverage and to the properties of the detectable emission outside that selection. This joint analysis distinguishes outer regions increasingly failing the Balmer-detection criteria from inner regions where non-SF-selected emission remains prominent, and tests how the latter changes as the young stellar contribution weakens.

That statement describes the observational connection indicated by the current saved analyses. Its quantitative strength still depends on the comparison and selection checks documented in the earlier report. The fraction of individual regions that physically change from one state to another, a unique ionisation mechanism, and the time spent in each state remain separate questions. The Introduction below is organised to make this connection the natural next step after the existing literature.

## B. Revised manuscript Section 1: Introduction

The dependence of galaxy properties on environment is a fundamental constraint on galaxy evolution. Galaxies in dense environments are more frequently associated with early-type morphologies and reduced star formation activity than those in lower-density environments, as established by the morphology-density relation and subsequent spectroscopic studies of nearby galaxy populations ([Dressler 1980](https://doi.org/10.1086/157753); [Lewis et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-8711.2002.05558.x); [Gomez et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1086/345593)). Large surveys further show that both stellar mass and environment are associated with the probability of quenching, with environmental effects particularly relevant to satellite galaxies ([Peng et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/721/1/193), [2012](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/757/1/4); [Wetzel et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stt469)). These population trends establish the importance of environment, but do not uniquely identify how the star-forming interstellar medium (ISM) is transformed or how the decline proceeds within individual discs (see [Boselli & Gavazzi 2006](https://doi.org/10.1086/500691); [Cortese, Catinella & Smith 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/pasa.2021.18); [Bluck 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00159-026-00171-0), for reviews). Understanding the connection between environmental processing and quenching therefore requires spatially resolved measurements of the sites of recent star formation and of the gas that remains detectable as their contribution declines.

Several physical mechanisms can contribute to this transformation. Ram pressure exerted by the intracluster medium (ICM) can displace and remove galactic gas when the external pressure becomes sufficiently strong relative to the restoring force of the galaxy ([Gunn & Gott 1972](https://doi.org/10.1086/151605)). A reduction in gas accretion can instead suppress the replenishment of the star-forming reservoir, allowing continued consumption to lower subsequent star formation ([Larson, Tinsley & Caldwell 1980](https://doi.org/10.1086/157917)). Gravitational encounters and repeated tidal perturbations can also redistribute gas and alter galactic structure ([Moore et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1038/379613a0)). These processes can act together and need not affect all gas phases, radii, or stellar populations simultaneously. In particular, removal of an extended atomic reservoir, compression of retained gas, and disruption or depletion of molecular material can produce different local responses during the same environmental encounter ([Boselli et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201322313); [Boselli, Fossati & Sun 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00159-022-00140-3)). The resulting star formation history is consequently sensitive to the initial gas distribution, the structure of the galaxy, and its interaction history.

The distinction between gas removal and star formation quenching is particularly important when interpreting integrated observations. A galaxy can retain a star-forming inner disc after losing much of its outer gas, while a lower integrated SFR can reflect either reduced activity within the remaining star-forming component or a reduction in its spatial extent. Conversely, a temporary local enhancement can occur while the galaxy's total gas reservoir is being depleted. Empirical satellite histories allow a period of continued star formation before a more rapid decline, although the inferred timescales depend on the population and the definition of infall ([Wetzel et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stt469)). Spatially resolved measurements are therefore needed to determine which parts of a galaxy continue to form stars and how their activity differs from that in less affected systems.

The Virgo cluster provides an unusually detailed view of these processes. Its proximity allows the atomic, molecular, stellar, and ionised components to be studied on sub-kiloparsec scales across a wide range of environmental conditions. The VLA Imaging of Virgo Spirals in Atomic Gas survey (VIVA) revealed extended, asymmetric, and strongly truncated H I distributions ([Chung et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-6256/138/6/1741)). Combined modelling and projected phase-space analyses connect these morphologies to different interaction histories and stripping states ([Vollmer 2009](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/200911892); [Yoon et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aa6579)). These classifications provide a useful basis for comparing galaxies at different stages of environmental processing, although differences in orbit, orientation, internal structure, and gravitational interactions prevent the populations from being interpreted as a unique evolutionary track.

Halpha imaging has long demonstrated that the suppressed integrated star formation of Virgo spirals is often associated with truncated star-forming discs, while their inner regions can retain normal or enhanced activity ([Koopmann & Kenney 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1086/423190), [2004b](https://doi.org/10.1086/423191)). Stellar-population measurements provide complementary constraints on when star formation declined within stripped discs ([Crowl & Kenney 2008](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-6256/136/4/1623)). The Virgo Environmental Survey Tracing Ionised Gas Emission (VESTIGE) extends this view through deep, high-resolution narrow-band imaging ([Boselli et al. 2018a](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201732407)). Its observations establish the environmental dependence of the galaxy-integrated SFMS and Halpha luminosity function, as well as the loss of individual H II regions, especially beyond the effective radius ([Boselli et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201937310), [2023a](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202244267), [2023b](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202346506)). Thus, the spatial disappearance of SF sites is an established part of the environmental picture.

The interpretation of truncation nevertheless depends on its degree and physical context. The detailed reconstruction of NGC 4330 links a rapid decline in star formation to a radially structured stripping history ([Fossati et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201732373)). Across the wider Virgo population, however, moderate truncations also occur far from the cluster centre. [Morgan et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202449225) shows that reduced gas supply can produce such truncations when a threshold for star formation is included, motivating an early starvation phase followed by stronger ram pressure. The radial extent of star formation should therefore be considered together with gas morphology and stellar history, rather than treated as a unique discriminator between gas-supply suppression and direct stripping.

The intensity of the remaining star-forming component adds an independent observational dimension. Regional luminosity, size, and density measurements in VESTIGE provide a baseline for this comparison ([Boselli et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202450963)). The extension to perturbed galaxies finds lower brightest-region luminosities at fixed host stellar properties, together with changes in the composite luminosity function that do not imply a uniform fading of all regions ([Boselli et al. 2026a](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202659463)). Furthermore, compact star-forming discs in Virgo lenticulars can exhibit approximately normal SF surface brightness despite their reduced integrated activity ([Boselli et al. 2022a](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202142482)). These results emphasise the need to distinguish a reduction in the number or extent of SF sites from a reduction in their conditional intensity. They also require explicit attention to the quantity held fixed: the luminosity of a segmented H II region at fixed host mass is not equivalent to the local SFR surface density at fixed stellar surface density.

The molecular-gas observations offer a complementary explanation of these spatial changes. The Virgo Environment Traced in CO survey (VERTICO; [Brown et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/ac28f5)) shows that environmental effects extend beyond the atomic phase. Molecular discs become more truncated and lose low-surface-density material in H I-deficient systems, while the retained inner gas can also have lower surface densities ([Zabel et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac6e68); [Watts et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1017/pasa.2023.14)). At the same time, changes in the efficiency with which molecular gas forms stars contribute to suppression ([Villanueva et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac9d3c); [Jimenez-Donaire et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202244718)). In particular, the resolved analysis of [Brown et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/acf195) connects lower SFR surface density at fixed stellar surface density to both reduced molecular content and lower molecular SF efficiency. Consequently, neither the presence of detectable gas nor a familiar ensemble gas-SFR relation guarantees that the local star-forming process is unaffected by environment.

Integral-field spectroscopy (IFS) makes it possible to examine these changes through the resolved star formation main sequence (rSFMS), which relates the SFR surface density to the underlying stellar mass surface density. This relation has been measured on kiloparsec scales in CALIFA, SAMI, MaNGA, and GASP, with substantial variation associated with galaxy properties and local structure ([Cano-Diaz et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8205/821/2/L26); [Medling et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/sty127); [Ellison et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stx2882); [Vulcani et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz1829)). It provides a local reference against which suppression and enhancement can be measured at comparable stellar surface density. However, a relation constructed only from SF-selected spaxels describes the component that satisfies that selection. It does not, by itself, determine how much of the surrounding disc has ceased to meet the same criteria.

Environmental IFS studies already demonstrate that both the strength and radial structure of SF can vary between populations. SAMI measurements connect dense environments to more centrally concentrated SF ([Schaefer et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw2289)). MaNGA analyses find satellite suppression across radii and stronger environmental effects in some low-mass satellite populations, while separating the properties associated with local SF intensity from those associated with quenching ([Spindler et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/sty247); [Lian et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz2218); [Bluck et al. 2020a](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz3264), [2020b](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staa2806)). More recently, [Cakir et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1017/pasa.2026.10157) finds increasingly suppressed outer SF in asymmetric and truncated SAMI cluster galaxies, with central activity comparable to that of less perturbed systems. These studies provide important precedents for a stage-dependent analysis, while differing in spatial resolution, sample selection, and the treatment of regions with weak emission lines.

Nearby MUSE surveys now allow the rSFMS to be examined on scales approaching individual star-forming complexes. The MUSE Atlas of Disks (MAD) and PHANGS-MUSE resolve nearby discs at scales of order 100 pc or smaller ([Erroz-Ferrer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz194); [Emsellem et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141727)). At these scales, spatial sampling and the different lifetimes of gas and SF tracers become important ([Kruijssen & Longmore 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stu098)). Using PHANGS data, [Pessa et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202140733) demonstrates that completeness and the treatment of non-detections change the measured resolved scaling relations and their apparent scale dependence. Comparisons must therefore account for the physical resolution, averaging procedure, diffuse-emission treatment, and selected footprint. An overlap between pooled distributions need not imply that the underlying environmental populations have identical conditional SF intensities or SF coverage.

Local enhancement introduces a further dimension to this comparison. Studies of the Gas Stripping Phenomena in galaxies with MUSE survey (GASP) find elevated integrated and spatially resolved SF in selected stripping galaxies ([Vulcani et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/aae68b), [2020b](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aba4ae)). HST imaging also reveals clumps whose Halpha luminosities at a given size exceed those of comparison regions in ordinary local star-forming galaxies ([Giunchi et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/acc5ee)). In Virgo, the compressed regions of IC 3476 and NGC 4654 provide resolved examples of enhanced activity ([Boselli et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202039046); [Lizee et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202038910)). VERTICO additionally finds excess molecular gas and SF on the leading sides of H I-tailed galaxies without requiring a shorter depletion time ([Roberts et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202346599)). Local SF enhancement can therefore reflect changes in available fuel, conversion efficiency, or both.

Such enhancement is not a universal property of environmentally affected galaxies. Earlier Virgo observations find no general increase in molecular SF efficiency ([Vollmer et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201118690)), and recent GASP molecular measurements show normal-to-low efficiencies with strong spatial dependence ([Moretti et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202558175)). A large UNIONS satellite sample shows no systematic asymmetric enhancement, while TNG50 simulations distinguish bursts along individual histories from an enhancement of the observed population as a whole ([Foster et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13123); [Goller et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09199)). GASP also contains abruptly quenched systems and galaxies with strongly truncated gas discs, demonstrating the importance of following the population beyond the conspicuous stripping phase ([Vulcani et al. 2020a](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ab7bdd); [Poggianti et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202554200)). A search for local excess is therefore most informative when embedded within an analysis of the wider progression towards suppression.

As the coverage of SF-selected emission decreases, the ionised medium need not simply disappear from the observed footprint. Some locations fall below the sensitivity of the observations, whereas others retain detectable line emission with different excitation properties. Extended low-ionisation emission-line regions (LIERs) occur in both star-forming and quiescent galaxies, and can trace distributed ionising sources rather than a central active galactic nucleus ([Belfiore et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw1234)). The resolved transition towards quiescence consequently involves spatially distinct combinations of SF, LIER-like, and weak or undetected emission ([Belfiore et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3211)). These established configurations motivate an environmental census that considers detectable emission outside the SF selection together with the SF component and the non-detections.

The physical interpretation of this census requires care because an excitation class is not a unique gas phase or energy source. Diffuse ionised gas (DIG) is widespread in galactic discs and can be maintained by ionising radiation escaping from young stellar populations, with additional contributions depending on the local conditions ([Haffner et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1103/RevModPhys.81.969)). At low Halpha surface brightness, the relative strengths of low-ionisation forbidden lines commonly increase, affecting the position of regions in emission-line diagnostic diagrams ([Zhang et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3308)). The measured mixture also depends on aperture and spatial resolution ([Mannucci et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab2648)). Therefore, a change in diagnostic ratios must be interpreted alongside the absolute line surface brightness and the spatial distribution of the emission.

Equivalent width adds information about the ionising output relative to the underlying stellar continuum. Weak Halpha emission associated with old stellar populations can occupy regions of diagnostic diagrams commonly attributed to non-stellar ionisation in unresolved observations ([Stasinska et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3933.2008.00550.x); [Cid Fernandes et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2011.18244.x)). Resolved studies distinguish diffuse and mixed components across the Hubble sequence using equivalent width and related diagnostics ([Lacerda et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stx3022)). However, no single equivalent-width or surface-brightness threshold universally isolates DIG in stripped galaxies ([Tomicic et al. 2021a](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abca93)). A measured decline in equivalent width is thus informative about the changing prominence of line emission, but does not alone identify its source.

Hot low-mass evolved stars, including post-asymptotic giant branch populations, provide a plausible distributed ionising field where recent SF is weak ([Flores-Fajardo et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2011.18848.x)). Stellar-population and photoionisation calculations predict weak Halpha emission and LIER-like line ratios under suitable gas conditions ([Byler et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ab1b70)). In PHANGS-MUSE, [Belfiore et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141859) investigates the relative contributions of leaked radiation and evolved stars to DIG. More recently, [Baron et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07163) proposes that propagation and spectral hardening of leaked radiation can explain important sequences connecting H II regions and diffuse emission in actively star-forming discs. These alternatives make the spatial relationship to SF, the stellar population, and the available photon budget essential to interpreting harder emission in an environmentally suppressed population.

Studies of stripped galaxies provide further constraints. GASP measurements find no clear stripped-control difference in the integrated DIG Halpha contribution, despite a wide range of diffuse fractions ([Tomicic et al. 2021a](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abca93)). This concerns an emission fraction rather than an area fraction. Diffuse tails can also exhibit excess [O I]/Halpha requiring additional excitation, potentially involving shocks or interactions with the hot surrounding medium ([Fossati et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stv2400); [Tomicic et al. 2021b](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac230e)). Interface-region spectroscopy connects changing excitation and SF activity to gas-disc truncation and stripping phase ([Pedrini et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stac345)). These results establish that environmental excitation changes are already observed, while leaving open how they relate quantitatively to the changing coverage and brightness of emission retained within galactic discs.

Tracers sensitive to different stellar timescales provide a complementary perspective on this retained emission. Comparisons of UV and Halpha in late-stage GASP galaxies associate their discrepancies in DIG-dominated regions with recently declining SF, while also identifying ionised regions with little evidence for an accompanying young UV population ([Tomicic et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ad7130)). In older Virgo systems, UV emission itself can have a substantial contribution from evolved populations ([Martocchia et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14956)). Such observations emphasise that the presence of a tracer is not equivalent to an ongoing SFR without an appropriate source model. The changing ionised-emission census must therefore be interpreted in conjunction with its spectral properties and stellar context.

Gas kinematics and density-sensitive line ratios provide additional constraints. Resolved MaNGA measurements connect broader gas profiles with non-H II-like excitation ([Law et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abfe0a)), while shock models demonstrate how mechanical energy can modify optical line spectra ([Allen et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1086/589652)). Nevertheless, an observed line width can contain unresolved velocity structure as well as local gas motions. Similarly, the [S II] doublet measures the density of the emitting component under adopted atomic and thermal conditions, with limited sensitivity in the low-density regime ([Kewley et al. 2019a](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ab16ed); [Barnes et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab2958)). These measurements are most informative when considered jointly with surface brightness, equivalent width, and line ratios, rather than as unique indicators of an environmental mechanism (see also [Kewley, Nicholls & Sutherland 2019](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-astro-081817-051832)).

The Multiphase Astrophysics to Unveil the Virgo Environment survey (MAUVE) was designed to connect the stellar and gaseous components of Virgo galaxies through spatially resolved observations ([Catinella et al. 2025](https://doi.eso.org/10.18727/0722-6691/5393)). In Paper I, we used MAUVE-MUSE observations to examine the local relation between metallicity and SF, and developed a resolved gas-regulator interpretation of the competition between SF-driven and gas-supply-driven variability ([Huang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stag1019)). Complementary MAUVE work already finds environmental differences in ionised-gas excitation and kinematics, interpreting the increased prominence of DIG primarily in terms of suppressed SF, with a secondary contribution from more strongly disturbed gas ([Brown et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae632e)). The next question is how this changing prominence is connected to the intensity and coverage of SF across the different environmental-stage populations.

In this work, we investigate that connection using the MAUVE-MUSE observations at physical resolutions of order 100 pc. We first compare the resolved SF distribution with nearby MUSE measurements and examine the conditional intensity of SF in the adopted pre-peak, close-to-peak, and post-peak populations, including the possibility of local excess. We then measure the fractions of the usable footprint classified as SF, non-detected in the required Balmer lines (ND), or detected but outside the adopted SF selection (NSF). This division allows us to distinguish the spatial loss of SF-selected emission from changes in its intensity, and to determine where detectable NSF emission accompanies lower SF coverage. Finally, we examine the absolute line surface brightness, diagnostic ratios, equivalent width, and supplementary gas properties of the NSF-selected component. A restricted gas-supply framework is used to investigate whether the combined behaviour is consistent with declining young-star emission and a changing mixture of ionising sources. Our aim is to establish the spatial connection among these observables, while distinguishing the measured population trends from a unique history for individual gas regions.

This paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the observations, derived quantities, environmental-stage assignments, emission classifications, and comparison methodology. Section 3 presents the resolved SF distributions, their stage dependence, and the local excess analysis. Section 4 examines the spatial census of SF, NSF, and ND regions. Section 5 characterises the detectable emission outside the SF selection. Section 6 develops the physical interpretation, compares it with previous work, and discusses the limitations of the observations and model. Section 7 presents our conclusions.
