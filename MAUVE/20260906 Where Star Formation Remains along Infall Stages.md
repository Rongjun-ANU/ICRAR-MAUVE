# 20260906 Where Star Formation Remains along Infall Stages

First of all, instead of a cut at stellar mass and radius, we perform a cut at `POSTFIT_SNR>25` to remove those faint, unreliable spaxels. We also perform a equal-galaxy plots, and the trends are similar. 

![image-20260906180139495](assets/image-20260906180139495.png)

In general, outer low-$\Sigma_⋆$ regions mainly become ND, while central high-$\Sigma_⋆$ regions remain emission-line detected but are increasingly NSF:

1. Pre-peak to close-to-peak: outside-in erosion. The decline in $𝑓_\mathrm{SF}$ is larger at $1-2𝑅_𝑒$ than inside $𝑅_𝑒$, and this outer loss comes with increasing $𝑓_\mathrm{ND}$.
2. Close-to-peak to post-peak: a large loss of SF regions across the observed disc. NSF becomes dominant within $0.5R_e$ and ND dominates outside. 
3. Therefore, the full pre-to-post difference is stronger quenching outside but then substantial everywhere. Outside-in quenching plus disc-wide reduction is more accurate than either pure outside-in or  uniform quenching.

If we look at $f_\mathrm{SF}(R/R_e)$, we will find in pre-peak sample, it is $0.4\sim0.8$ with slightly positive slope across the disc up to $3R_e$. And in close-to-peak, its central value remains 0.5 and graduately declines and reaches 0 at $3R_e$. Then in post-peak, it is only 0.1 to 0.2 within $R_e$ and almost 0 SF outside $1.5R_e$. For $f_\mathrm{NSF}(R/R_e)$, it shows steeper decrease along infall stages and dominates within $0.5R_e$ in post-peak. Conversely, $f_\mathrm{ND}(R/R_e)$ becomes steeper increase in close-to-peak and post-peak, while only $\lesssim0.2$ in all radius in pre-peak.

$\Sigma_*$ tends also give a slightly different prospective. In pre-peak, ND is $\lesssim0.2$ and SF dominantes from 7.0 to 9.5. In close-to-peak, $f_\mathrm{ND}$ rises especially in low-mass end, while the sharp SF drop in $\log(\Sigma_*)>9.0$ goes to NSF. Then in post-peak, only $\lesssim20\%$ regions still SF, and concentrate between 7.5-9.0. ND dominates <8.25 and NSF dominates >8.25. And this 8.25 in stellar mass transition seems to correlate with trainsition at $0.6R_e$. 

SF becoming ND in outer low-$\Sigma_⋆$ has a natural RPS interpretation. Since those regions have lower $\Sigma_⋆$ and $\Sigma_\mathrm{gas}$, and so a shallower local gravitational potential/restoring force, then the outside-in gas removal removes the conditions required to maintain H II regions. Therefore, rather than changing the excitation mechanism of the outer gas, those outer low-$\Sigma_⋆$ regions lose sufficiently strong nebular emission. The pre-peak to close-to-peak transition further support this explanation. The close-to-peak samples still maintain $f_\mathrm{SF}\gtrsim0.4$ up to $2R_e$, but quickly drop to near 0 at $2.3R_e$. 

SF becoming NDF in inner high-$\Sigma_⋆$ means the presence of ionized gas but the decrease of O/B star dominance. This can be ionization due to shocks/outflows, AGN, DIG, older stellar population, or bulge/nuclear radiation. And we can do a simple classification based on their morphology. For those extraplanar NSF regions (like NGC4064), we know those are starburst outflows; for those NSF regions surround or bridge SF regions (like IC3392), they should be DIG or leaked OB photons; and for those central NSF regions (like NGC4698), they are likely old-star LINER and/or AGN; also there are maybe mixed situations (like NGC4580). 

One interesting thing is that in pre-peak and post-peak, both stellar and radial trends show consistent information, which follows the radial profile of stellar mass,  but the close-to-peak samples do not act as a clear transition and behave differently in two trends. It only shows the absolute dominance of ND in outer radius, but not necessarily in low mass end; while it only shows the absolute dominance of NSF in high mass end but still relatively higher fraction of SF in inner disc. I think this may due to the fact that our close-to-peak samples are not representative enough (only 5 galaxies here after inclination selection). But i think this may also suggest that ND appears more directly linked to stripping geometry, whereas NSF appears more linked to the local stellar/ionization population. This may also confirm that there is no such a clear intermediate transition phase from pre-peak to post-peak. Iin fact, the spatial maps of close-to-peak samples also support this: some close-to-peak galaxies remain blue-rich with orange ND stay mainly at their edges (NGC4298, NGC4501 and NGC4654), while others have compact central SF embedded in NSF and surrounded by ND (NGC4424 and NGC4694).
