## A. The argument that should organise Paper II

### A1. Central question and proposed title

The strongest organising question is: **How does the spatial decline of star formation connect to the disappearance, persistence, and changing excitation of detectable ionised emission across the MAUVE infall-stage populations?** This makes the SF and NSF results parts of one investigation. The rSFMS establishes the behaviour of the regions still selected as star forming. The SF/NSF/ND census then establishes where that selection ceases to occupy the observed disc. Finally, the NSF diagnostics describe the emission that remains detectable in those populations.

A suitable working title is:

**MAUVE-MUSE: the spatial progression of star formation suppression and residual ionised emission in Virgo galaxies**

An alternative with a stronger emphasis on the transition is **MAUVE-MUSE: from declining star formation to residual ionised emission along the Virgo stripping sequence**. The first is preferable at this stage because it accommodates differences between galaxies without implying that individual regions have been followed through time. Neither title requires a secure electron-density trend or a unique identification of HOLMES as the ionising source.

The principal observational statement can be developed as follows:

> Across the adopted infall-stage populations, declining star formation is accompanied by different spatial changes in the emission census. The outer disc increasingly fails the adopted Balmer-detection requirements, whereas the inner disc retains a substantial component of detectable emission outside the adopted SF selection. The changing coverage of these components, together with the intensity of surviving SF and the declining brightness and equivalent width of NSF emission, provides a spatially resolved description of environmental suppression.

This statement is deliberately about a measured distribution. The interpretation that outer gas removal and inner changes in the ionising-source mixture contribute to that distribution belongs in Section 6. An observed NSF location is not a record of a particular H II region becoming diffuse gas, and an ND location is not by itself proof that all gas has been removed.

### A2. What is already established, and what this paper adds

Three strands of prior work provide the starting point. Virgo imaging and H I studies already establish truncated SF discs and outside-in environmental processing. Spatially resolved spectroscopy already establishes detectable LIER-like emission in regions with weak recent SF. Work on stripped galaxies, including MAUVE itself, already connects environmental disturbance with unusual optical line ratios and broader ionised-gas profiles. Paper II should acknowledge all three strands early and then bring them together through the same spatial census.

The most distinctive contribution is therefore the **joint description of SF intensity, SF/NSF/ND coverage, and the properties of the detectable NSF component as functions of local stellar surface density and radius across the adopted stages**. Its value is the connection between quantities that are often analysed separately. This is more specific, and more defensible, than claiming the first discovery of outside-in quenching, residual ionised gas, or local SF enhancement during stripping.

The exact combination has a credible claim to being a useful extension of earlier work, but the literature examined here does not justify an unrestricted priority claim. In particular, [Boselli et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201937310) already resolved the loss of H II regions in gas-stripped Virgo galaxies on approximately 50 pc scales, while [Belfiore et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3211) established central and extended LIER configurations. These precedents leave room for a joint environmental analysis, rather than an assertion that either spatial outcome was previously unknown.

### A3. The logical progression

The seven sections can be retained with only the numbering correction that observations and methods constitute Section 2. The progression is: establish the comparison and measurement definitions; describe surviving SF and the NGC 4654 exception; account for the spatial coverage lost from the SF category; characterise the residual detected emission; and interpret the connected measurements with a restricted physical model.

The NGC 4654 analysis should remain a focused subsection of Section 3. It establishes that local excess and broader suppression can coexist, but it should not displace the SF/NSF/ND transition as the principal result. Similarly, velocity dispersion and the [S II] doublet should test the interpretation without becoming conditions on which the entire paper depends.

## B. Draft manuscript Section 1: Introduction

The decline of star formation in satellite galaxies is closely connected to the interaction between their gas reservoirs and the surrounding environment. In galaxy clusters, ram pressure exerted by the hot intracluster medium can displace and remove interstellar gas, while a reduction in subsequent gas supply can limit the replenishment of material consumed by star formation. These processes need not affect all gas phases or all locations within a galaxy simultaneously. Consequently, the relation between gas removal and star formation quenching depends on the structure of the galaxy, the distribution of its gas, and its environmental history ([Gunn & Gott 1972](https://doi.org/10.1086/151605); [Cortese, Catinella & Smith 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02193); [Boselli, Fossati & Sun 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00159-022-00140-3)). Understanding this connection requires measurements that resolve both the sites of ongoing star formation and the surrounding interstellar medium as the relative importance of young stellar populations declines.

The Virgo cluster provides a particularly useful environment in which to investigate these processes. Its proximity allows the distributions of atomic gas, recent star formation, and optical line emission to be compared within individual galactic discs. Halpha imaging demonstrated that the reduced integrated star formation of many Virgo spirals is associated with truncated star-forming discs, while their inner regions can retain substantial activity ([Koopmann & Kenney 2004](https://doi.org/10.1086/423190)). At finer spatial resolution, the Virgo Environmental Survey Tracing Ionised Gas Emission (VESTIGE) showed that gas-stripped galaxies contain fewer H II regions and that this deficit becomes particularly pronounced outside the effective radius ([Boselli et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201937310)). These observations establish that environmental suppression has a spatial structure that cannot be recovered from an integrated star formation rate alone. They also motivate a further question: what distinguishes the regions where star-forming emission is no longer detected from those where ionised emission remains visible after the contribution from recent star formation has weakened?

The atomic-gas morphology provides an independent basis for relating these spatial changes to environmental processing. The VLA Imaging of Virgo Spirals in Atomic Gas survey (VIVA) revealed a wide range of H I configurations, including compressed edges, asymmetric extensions, and severely truncated discs ([Chung et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-6256/138/6/1741)). Combining such information with projected phase space provides constraints on the likely stripping histories of Virgo galaxies ([Yoon et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aa6579)). However, a population classified by stripping state does not constitute a direct time sequence of otherwise identical galaxies. Differences in orbital geometry, gravitational interactions, and pre-existing structure can produce different responses at comparable environmental stages. A stage-based comparison is therefore most informative when it is interpreted as a comparison of spatially resolved populations, with the physical sequence constrained by additional gas and stellar information.

Gas removal can influence star formation through more than one route. In a retained molecular reservoir, reduced replenishment can lower the subsequent star formation rate without requiring an immediate change in the efficiency with which molecular gas forms stars. Conversely, changes in the molecular content, its dynamical state, or its conversion into stars can alter the relation between the gas reservoir and the observed star formation. A first-order correspondence between molecular-gas and star formation surface densities is observed in nearby discs, although the depletion time varies with environment and physical scale ([Leroy et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-6256/146/2/19)). In Virgo, VERTICO measurements link environmental suppression to changes in both molecular-gas content and star formation efficiency ([Brown et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/acf195)). These results show why a decline in star formation alone cannot identify which part of the gas cycle has changed.

Integral-field spectroscopy enables this problem to be approached through the resolved star formation main sequence (rSFMS), which relates the local star formation rate surface density to the underlying stellar mass surface density. The relation has been established on approximately kiloparsec scales in CALIFA and extended to scales of order 100 pc with nearby MUSE surveys ([Cano-Diaz et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8205/821/2/L26); [Erroz-Ferrer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stz194)). It provides a reference against which local suppression and excess can be measured at comparable stellar surface density. However, its interpretation depends on which locations enter the measurement. A relation constructed from detected, SF-selected regions describes the surviving star-forming component, whereas an average across a larger disc footprint also depends on how much of that footprint contains such emission.

This distinction becomes especially relevant near the scale of individual star-forming complexes. Using PHANGS data, [Pessa et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202140733) demonstrated that the treatment of non-detections changes the measured rSFMS and its apparent dependence on spatial scale. Their work provides a particularly relevant comparison for MAUVE because the surveys probe similar physical scales with MUSE. It also shows that a comparison must account for spatial averaging, emission selection, and the quantity being fitted. Similarity in the overall rSFMS distribution can coexist with differences in the frequency and spatial distribution of star-forming locations. The rSFMS and the coverage of SF-selected emission therefore provide complementary information on the environmental response.

Observational studies also show that environmental processing need not produce uniform suppression at every location. The GAs Stripping Phenomena in galaxies with MUSE survey (GASP) has reported enhanced resolved star formation in selected stripping galaxies, while Virgo studies of molecular-gas efficiency do not find a universal enhancement on the windward side of stripped discs ([Vulcani et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aba4ae); [Vollmer et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201118690)). The recent SAMI analysis of ram-pressure-affected cluster populations likewise finds spatial differences between asymmetric and truncated systems, with suppression most evident at low stellar surface density and large radius ([Cakir et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03088)). These findings are not described by a single expectation for all galaxies labelled as stripped. The measured outcome depends on the selected population, the stage of the interaction, and whether the comparison concerns local star formation, molecular-gas efficiency, or an integrated rate.

Individual Virgo galaxies illustrate the additional information supplied by spatial geometry. NGC 4654 has a well-established combination of tidal and hydrodynamical perturbations, and its compressed gas region has previously been associated with enhanced star formation efficiency ([Vollmer 2003](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361:20021729); [Lizee et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202038910)). A directional optical excess in this galaxy can therefore be compared with an independently inferred gas-compression geometry, while allowing for a contribution from its tidal history. By contrast, the centrally concentrated activity in NGC 4424 occurs in a system for which a merger-related starburst and outflow have been identified alongside stripping ([Boselli et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201833914)). These cases motivate a separation between the statistical behaviour of the stage populations and the physical interpretation of particular local enhancements.

The loss of SF-selected regions does not imply that optical line emission must disappear at the same locations or on the same timescale. Diffuse ionised gas can be maintained by photons escaping from H II regions, by hot low-mass evolved stars (HOLMES), or by additional sources of heating and ionisation. Spatially resolved MaNGA observations established that low-ionisation emission-line regions (LIERs) are widespread outside galactic nuclei and can be associated with old stellar populations ([Belfiore et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw1234)). Central and extended LIER configurations further demonstrate that weak recent star formation can coexist with detectable ionised gas ([Belfiore et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3211)). Thus, the persistence of non-H II-like emission is already an established phenomenon. The environmental question is how its spatial prominence changes alongside the loss of star-forming emission and the growth of non-detections within the same observed disc.

Equivalent width and emission-line ratios offer complementary constraints on this residual emission. The equivalent width of Halpha compares nebular emission with the underlying stellar continuum and therefore helps distinguish strong young-star-powered emission from weak emission associated with older populations ([Cid Fernandes et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2011.18244.x); [Lacerda et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stx3022)). Meanwhile, diffuse gas at low Halpha surface brightness commonly exhibits elevated low-ionisation forbidden-line-to-Balmer ratios ([Zhang et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stw3308)). Such behaviour means that weaker absolute line emission and larger diagnostic ratios can occur together. It also means that a location outside an adopted SF selection cannot be assigned a unique ionising source from its classification alone. The relation between surface brightness, equivalent width, and excitation must be examined explicitly.

The origin of diffuse emission remains sensitive to the local radiation field and gas geometry. In PHANGS-MUSE galaxies, [Belfiore et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141859) examined a combination of leaking radiation from H II regions and ionisation by evolved stars. More recently, [Baron et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07163) argued that the propagation and spectral hardening of leaked radiation can explain important properties of diffuse emission in actively star-forming discs. The latter study is currently a preprint and provides a relevant alternative to assigning all harder diffuse emission to HOLMES. Stellar-population and photoionisation calculations nevertheless show that post-AGB populations can produce weak Halpha emission with LIER-like ratios ([Byler et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ab1b70)). An environmentally processed sample spanning strong and weak recent SF can therefore help constrain where these explanations remain plausible, provided that photon production and gas absorption are considered separately.

Previous studies of stripped systems provide both guidance and important qualifications. [Tomicic et al. (2021a)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abca93) found no clear difference in the inferred integrated DIG contribution between their stripped and control samples. This result concerns an emission fraction and does not directly determine the fraction of disc area assigned to a non-SF category. In addition, diffuse gas in stripped tails can exhibit excitation requiring processes beyond ordinary star formation, potentially including shocks or mixing with the surrounding medium ([Tomicic et al. 2021b](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac230e)). Those tail conditions need not apply to emission retained in the inner disc. Comparisons of UV and Halpha in late-stage GASP galaxies also demonstrate that diffuse emission can be associated with a recent decline in star formation, highlighting the value of tracers with different temporal sensitivities ([Tomicic et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ad7130)).

Ionised-gas kinematics and density-sensitive line ratios provide additional tests of the physical interpretation. The association between broader emission lines and non-H II-like excitation is well established in resolved spectroscopic surveys ([Law et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/abfe0a)). However, an observed line width can contain contributions from unresolved velocity structure as well as local gas motions. Similarly, the [S II] doublet probes the density of the gas contributing to its emission and becomes weakly sensitive to density near its low-density limit ([Kewley et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ab16ed); [Barnes et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stab2958)). These diagnostics are therefore most useful when interpreted jointly with the spatial distribution and strength of the emission. They can test a proposed explanation without requiring every change in excitation to correspond to a unique change in turbulence or electron density.

MAUVE-MUSE was designed to investigate the gas cycle in nearby Virgo galaxies using spatially resolved observations of their stellar and gaseous components ([Catinella et al. 2025](https://doi.eso.org/10.18727/0722-6691/5393)). In Paper I, we used MAUVE observations to investigate the relation between local metallicity and star formation, and developed a resolved regulator framework for interpreting the balance between star formation and gas-supply variability ([Huang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stag1019)). Complementary MAUVE work has already established environmental differences in the excitation and kinematics of the ionised medium relative to the PHANGS comparison sample ([Brown et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae632e)). These studies motivate a more explicit connection between the behaviour of the remaining star-forming regions and the spatially resolved emission census across different stages of environmental processing.

In this work, we investigate that connection using the MAUVE-MUSE data at physical resolutions of order 100 pc. We first place the resolved SF distribution in the context of nearby MUSE measurements and examine its dependence on the adopted pre-peak, close-to-peak, and post-peak populations. We then distinguish the intensity of surviving SF from the fractions of the usable footprint classified as SF, non-detected (ND), or detected but outside the adopted SF selection (NSF). This division allows us to ask where declining SF coverage is accompanied predominantly by a loss of detectable Balmer emission, and where detectable NSF emission remains prominent. Finally, we examine the surface brightness, line ratios, equivalent width, and supplementary gas diagnostics of the NSF population, and interpret their combined behaviour using a restricted model that links declining gas supply to changing ionising-source contributions. The aim is to establish how the different observed components are connected spatially, while distinguishing that observational connection from a unique evolutionary history or ionisation mechanism.

This paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the observations, derived quantities, stage assignments, and emission classifications. Section 3 presents the resolved SF comparison, the stage dependence of surviving SF, and the local excess in NGC 4654. Section 4 examines the spatial distribution of SF, NSF, and ND regions. Section 5 characterises the emission that remains detectable outside the SF selection. Section 6 develops the physical interpretation, compares it with previous work, and discusses the limitations of the model and observations. Section 7 presents our conclusions.

For a galaxy g in a fixed stellar-density interval, define the SF occupancy O_g and the SF-selected luminosity or SFR contribution per usable area T_g. The current stage estimator can then be expressed as

$$O_g=N_{{\rm SF},g}/N_{{\rm usable},g},\qquad T_g=\sum_{i\in{\rm SF},g}\Sigma_{{\rm SFR},i}/N_{{\rm usable},g}.$$

$$O=\langle O_g\rangle_g,\qquad T=\langle T_g\rangle_g,\qquad I_{\rm SF}=T/O.$$

This identity is the transition to Section 4: the SF-selected contribution can fall because the surviving SF is weaker, because SF occupies less usable area, or because both change. ### C4. Section 5: The properties of detectable NSF emission

**5.1 Absolute line surface brightness and relative line ratios.** Place the brightness and ratio results next to one another. ## D. The comparison with previous work: what is established and what remains distinctive

### D1. The rSFMS comparison is useful, but its present interpretation should be narrower

PHANGS is the most directly useful large MUSE comparison for this analysis, but it is neither the only survey approaching 100 pc resolution nor a purely field control. The MAD results of Erroz-Ferrer et al. (2019) provide another high-resolution precedent. The Pessa et al. (2021) sample includes Virgo members and overlaps the MAUVE comparison through NGC 4254, NGC 4321, and NGC 4535. Calling the comparison "MAUVE versus nearby star-forming PHANGS galaxies" avoids attributing every difference, or lack of difference, to a clean cluster-field contrast. The overlap should be listed and removed in an independent-control sensitivity check.

Pessa et al. used spatially sampled maps at several physical scales, included zero-valued non-detections in linear averaging, and fitted binned relations. Their emission treatment also includes a DIG correction. The current MAUVE notebook instead displays an upper-limit construction: stored ND limits are included, and NSF Halpha is assigned an SFR-equivalent value under full young-star attribution. This is a deliberately generous observable proxy. It is not the same estimator as the PHANGS measurement, and it is not a censored-data likelihood. The distinction concerns both the meaning of an individual value and the way the values are averaged.

The current notebook's SF-only equal-galaxy individual-spaxel OLS has slope approximately 0.984 and intercept -10.034, while the displayed PHANGS relation has slope 1.05 and intercept -10.63 in the adopted units. These numbers do not support a statement of equal fitted relations: they refer to different selections and estimators, and slope and intercept cannot be compared independently over a broad mass range. The closer agreement of the displayed upper-limit mean curve over intermediate stellar density is a separate visual observation. Nor does overlap in a dense portion of the plot demonstrate equality of the full two-dimensional distributions.

There is nevertheless a valuable result to preserve. A familiar local SF locus can be populated by galaxies with very different fractions of their observed discs still meeting the SF criteria. The global comparison motivates the occupancy analysis rather than resolving the environmental question by itself. A manuscript-ready statement at the current evidence level is:

> The MAUVE emission distributions overlap the region occupied by nearby PHANGS galaxies, while the stage-dependent measurements reveal differences within the MAUVE population. Because the treatment of non-SF emission and non-detections differs between the surveys, this comparison establishes broad consistency in the sampled parameter space rather than equality of the underlying rSFMS.

The immediate refinement is to reproduce the same linear estimator on a common physical scale and stellar-density interval, document the treatment of missing and censored values, and compare independent galaxies. Matching effective resolution means matching the PSF and spatial averaging, not simply using native pixels of similar angular size. This report does not assume that the existing native-spaxel maps have already been convolved to a common 100 pc beam. Any censored-data analysis must also account for the way Hbeta detectability, dust correction, and SF classification enter the selection; replacing every limit by zero or by its upper bound provides different descriptive brackets, not a complete statistical solution.

### D2. The stage-dependent SF result extends an established environmental pattern

The relevant starting point is not that local SF suppression has never been demonstrated in clusters. Brown et al. (2023) already found a suppressed Virgo rSFMS on approximately 720 pc scales and connected the offset to molecular-gas content and efficiency. Their measurements also allow local enhancement in the outskirts of early-stage systems. Cakir et al. (2026) found stronger outer and low-density suppression in disturbed and truncated SAMI galaxies, with broadly comparable central SF across their classes. These studies support the spatial logic of the MAUVE interpretation while showing that the use of environmental categories alone is not the novel element.

MAUVE adds finer spatial discrimination and a consistent description of how detectable SF intensity, SF occupancy, and the alternative emission categories vary together. Differences from GASP should be presented as a comparison of sample selection, resolution, stage definition, and observable. A higher SFR at fixed stellar density is not the same measurement as higher SFR per unit molecular gas. Vulcani et al. (2020) and Vollmer et al. (2012) therefore need not disagree simply because one finds enhanced resolved SF in a selected stripping population and the other does not find a general increase in windward molecular-gas efficiency.

A useful discussion sentence is: "The coexistence of local enhancement with declining stage-averaged SF is consistent with a spatially heterogeneous response to gas compression and removal; the prevalence and amplitude of each response depend on the population and scale being measured." This formulation accommodates the NGC 4654 result without requiring it to explain the majority of the sample.

Its ionised extension is directed differently from the long atomic-gas tail. The central SF excess is consequently not a clean tracer of a disc-plane, windward enhancement. It is appropriate to retain this galaxy in the candidate census and explain why it is excluded from the particular directional test. ### D5. Fading NSF emission, EW, dispersion, and density

The expected comparison for declining EW is the WHAN, MaNGA LIER, and CALIFA DIG literature. Those studies establish why weak Halpha relative to an old stellar continuum is compatible with a reduced young-star contribution. They do not imply that every MAUVE NSF spaxel is a retired region. The saved pre-peak NSF profiles include EW values well above commonly used retired-galaxy thresholds; the current NSF category can be entered through kinematics or BPT selection even when EW is substantial. The thresholds of Cid Fernandes et al. (2011), the regimes of Lacerda et al. (2018), and the notebook's 6 Angstrom cut serve different classifications and should not be substituted for one another.

Zhang et al. (2017) and the PHANGS DIG studies establish that lower surface brightness and elevated line ratios occur together outside a cluster-stage analysis. The MAUVE contribution is the dependence of those joint measurements on the environmental populations and location within the disc. An increase in a line ratio does not require an increase in the forbidden-line luminosity. For the current profiles, the physical question is whether Balmer emission fades more strongly than the forbidden lines on comparable, adequately detected samples.

The saved [N II] dispersion profiles are suggestive in the centres but do not define a monotonic sequence at all radii and stellar densities. The environmental interpretation must be compared with the general excitation-width association established by Law et al. (2021) and the direct MAUVE results of Brown et al. (2026). Using [N II] reduces the thermal contribution relative to hydrogen, but subtracting an instrumental width alone does not remove unresolved rotation, multiple components, or all thermal broadening. Moreover, the SF/NSF classification still uses Halpha width; correlated or tied gas kinematics can create a selection dependence in an NSF [N II] comparison. The line is not an independent classification-free test merely because it is a different species.

The current [S II] profiles are even less suited to a single stage statement. Many ratios lie near approximately 1.4-1.46, where the density diagnostic is close to its low-density limit for ordinary nebular conditions. The saved central post-peak value is approximately 1.22, below the pre-peak value of approximately 1.29 and the close-to-peak value of approximately 1.38; other intervals have different ordering. These are visual approximate values, without a new uncertainty calculation. They rule out describing the displayed result as a universal progression towards larger 6716/6731 ratios and lower density. Near the limit, a density upper bound or consistency with low density is more meaningful than a sharply measured small density.

For a galaxy g, let A_g and B_g be the summed 6716 and 6731 intensities over N_g accepted pixels. The notebook's equal-galaxy estimator is

$$R_{\rm eq}=\frac{\sum_g A_g/N_g}{\sum_g B_g/N_g}.$$

This is a ratio of equally weighted galaxy-mean intensities. It is not the arithmetic mean of individual ratios or densities. Even after converting a well-measured ratio with an assumed temperature and atomic dataset, the result characterises the sulfur-emitting gas and need not equal the volume-average density of all ionised material. Kewley et al. (2019) and Barnes et al. (2021) provide the appropriate diagnostic context; a proposed stage-dependent density decrease requires its own evidence.

## E. A physical discussion built from the 20 October model

### E1. The useful explanatory content of the gas-supply calculation

The existing note contains a useful physical argument: environmental reduction of an atomic reservoir can reduce the supply to the molecular phase, and hence the luminosity supplied by young stars, while a more slowly varying stellar component remains. The general gas-regulator context is established by [Lilly et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/772/2/119) and developed for a different MAUVE question in Paper I. In Paper II the calculation should be introduced as a minimal model of this one route to declining SF, with its additional radiation assumptions made explicit.

Let H(t) and M(t) denote the atomic and molecular reservoirs of a representative region. For constant coefficients, no external replenishment after the chosen onset, and no direct molecular stripping, the note's bookkeeping can be written as

$$\dot H=-aH,\qquad \dot M=H/\tau_{\rm conv}-bM,\qquad \psi=M/\tau_{\rm dep},$$

$$a=\tau_{\rm conv}^{-1}+\gamma_{\rm strip},\qquad b=(1-\mathcal{R}+\eta)/\tau_{\rm dep}.$$

Here psi is the SFR, R is the assumed promptly returned fraction, eta is the adopted mass-loading factor, and the treatment of recycling is part of the reservoir approximation. For a different from b,

$$H(t)=H_0e^{-at},\qquad M(t)=M_0e^{-bt}+\frac{H_0}{\tau_{\rm conv}}\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-bt}}{b-a}.$$

For a = b, the second solution becomes

$$M(t)=e^{-bt}\left(M_0+\frac{H_0t}{\tau_{\rm conv}}\right).$$

The initial derivative makes the role of the initial condition transparent: molecular gas initially grows only if H_0/tau_conv exceeds b M_0. An initially balanced reservoir has zero initial derivative and subsequently declines as its atomic supply falls. An initially oversupplied molecular reservoir can rise before declining. This alternative is an illustrative initial condition, not a fitted observation of a stripping-induced burst. Increasing atomic removal at fixed initial reservoirs reduces the later supply; the baseline equations contain no compression-induced change in molecular formation or SF efficiency with which to generate a positive RPS response.

The distinction matters for NGC 4654. Its local excess may motivate a model with compression, transport, or altered molecular conversion, but it is not explained simply by increasing the stripping coefficient in the fading model. Likewise, the model's time origin is an assumed change in supply. It is not automatically the time of peak ram pressure, pericentre passage, or maximum observed SF. The three observational stage labels should not be placed at fitted times without an independent calibration.

The spatial factor c_HI in the note rescales the initial atomic reservoir while holding other chosen quantities fixed. It should remain separate from the temporal initial-condition experiment. It is not a measured local stripping rate, a local clock, or evidence that a specified part of the galaxy was compressed. The observation that different choices produce plausible spatial trends demonstrates the flexibility of the interpretation; it does not identify which choice occurs in the data.

Finally, neglecting direct molecular stripping is a simplifying limit. Evidence that molecular gas can be environmentally affected exists in Virgo and elsewhere, including [Boselli et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201322313). The baseline also omits changes in depletion time, radial inflow, and a time-dependent wind. These omissions are acceptable for an explanatory model if its purpose is stated clearly and the unmodelled channels remain part of the discussion.

### E2. Why weaker lines and larger ratios can occur together

Let Y be the Halpha luminosity surface density powered by the young-star contribution and L_o the Halpha surface density powered by an alternative, relatively persistent component. The subscript o can denote an evolved-star component in the baseline interpretation; it should not imply that the data uniquely identify that component. If both components emit a forbidden line with fixed linear line ratios r_y and r_o relative to Halpha, then

$$I_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}=Y+L_o,\qquad I_\ell=r_yY+r_oL_o,$$

$$r_\ell=\frac{I_\ell}{I_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}}=(1-w)r_y+wr_o,\qquad w=\frac{L_o}{Y+L_o}.$$

If Y decreases while L_o stays approximately fixed, w increases. If r_o exceeds r_y, the observed ratio rises even though both Halpha and the forbidden line fade. The forbidden-line luminosity declines more slowly because its persistent component has the larger line ratio. No increase in the absolute power of that component is required. This is the key mechanism that can link the Section 4 census to the Section 5 brightness and ratio profiles.

Mix the linear intensities first and take logarithms afterwards. A straight interpolation between logarithmic BPT coordinates is not generally the same model. For [O III]/Hbeta, the mixing weight is set by each component's Hbeta contribution. It equals the Halpha-based weight only under the assumed common Balmer decrement. Different attenuation or intrinsic decrements require a separate Hbeta weight and should not be hidden by the plotting convention.

The simple prediction is conditional on r_o > r_y for the particular ratio. A harder source spectrum does not guarantee that every BPT ratio rises by the same amount, or even in the same direction, at arbitrary abundance, ionisation parameter, and gas geometry. Metallicity and N/O are particularly relevant to [N II]/Halpha, while [O III]/Hbeta also responds strongly to ionisation conditions. Paper I itself motivates care with abundance-related differences across the sample. A changing source mixture is a plausible explanation to be assessed against these alternatives, not a unique inversion from BPT position.

The baseline can also produce declining EW if the stellar continuum changes more slowly than the nebular luminosity. This supplies an additional qualitative connection. It does not predict an exact EW trajectory without a stellar-population and continuum model. The continuum and its age distribution can differ between the stage populations even at the same total stellar surface density.

### E3. Ionising photons must still encounter gas

The strongest correction to the physical interpretation is to distinguish the persistence of old stars from the persistence of line luminosity. A useful generalisation of the note's old-star term is

$$L_o=\epsilon_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}\,f_{{\rm abs},o}\,q_o\,\Sigma_{{\rm old}},$$

where q_o is the ionising-photon production rate per unit mass of the relevant evolved population, f_abs,o is the fraction producing the local nebular emission, and epsilon_Halpha is the adopted conversion from absorbed ionising photons to Halpha luminosity. The note adopts q_o = 7 x 10^40 s^-1 per solar mass and epsilon_Halpha = 1.3721 x 10^-12 erg per absorbed photon, giving approximately 9.60 x 10^28 erg/s per solar mass before multiplication by the absorption fraction and the appropriate stellar surface density. This is an adopted normalisation associated with the Belfiore et al. (2022) modelling context, not a MAUVE measurement or a universal constant of all old populations.

If gas is removed, its covering fraction falls, or photons travel beyond the local region, f_abs,o can decrease even when the evolved population is unchanged. Therefore, a persistent stellar photon source does not guarantee a fixed Halpha floor. In the innermost retained gas, roughly persistent absorption may be a useful approximation. In the outskirts, the same approximation can fail and line emission may become undetectable. This provides a coherent qualitative connection between the two observed outcomes, but it requires a spatial gas/absorption model to become a quantitative prediction.

The analogous young-star term also depends on absorption and photon transport. At approximately 100 pc scales, ionising stars and the gas that absorbs their radiation need not be co-spatial. The usual conversion between instantaneous local SFR and Halpha further assumes that the young population and its recent history are sufficiently well sampled. These issues affect the interpretation of a local residual relative to the rSFMS as well as the physical interpretation of NSF emission.

Recombination emission primarily constrains an emission measure, involving an integral of electron and proton densities along the line of sight. It does not directly measure the remaining ionised-gas mass without a geometry, filling factor, and density distribution. Thus neither a lower surface brightness nor an increased NSF area fraction alone establishes how much gas has been retained or removed.

### E4. Leakage, HOLMES, shocks, and mixed explanations

The paper should compare a small set of hypotheses through their expected combinations of observables. In an evolved-star interpretation, the declining young-star contribution reveals a component related to the old stellar population and constrained by its photon budget. In a leakage interpretation, the radiation field depends on nearby H II regions and transport through the disc. In a shock or mixing interpretation, additional heating and gas motions become relevant. Weak nuclear activity, unresolved components, abundance changes, and bulge-dominated stellar populations can also contribute locally. Several mechanisms can coexist.

Belfiore et al. (2022) and the new Baron et al. (2026) preprint provide a particularly useful contrast in how the radiation from young and evolved populations is assessed. Their results should be compared in the star-forming-disc regime actually studied, rather than treated as a single definitive prescription for advanced stripping populations. The recent preprint makes leaked and hardened radiation a substantive alternative. The weak-EW, evolved-population context of Byler et al. (2019) remains relevant where recent SF is small. The MAUVE data can test consistency with these regimes; the present measurements do not uniquely decompose them.

The extra leakage parameter in the 20 October note should describe the fraction of young-star photons absorbed in the diffuse component, for example f_leak,abs. It should not be called a measured galaxy-wide Lyman-continuum escape fraction. Photons escaping an H II region can still be absorbed within the galaxy; photons escaping the galaxy do not produce its local recombination emission. Likewise, a reduction in neutral column does not guarantee monotonically stronger spectral hardening. Filtering, the available photon budget, and absorption all change together and require a radiative-transfer prescription.

The practical tests are correspondingly focused: compare residual Halpha with an old-population photon budget and stellar age indicators; compare diffuse emission with nearby H II-region geometry; inspect nuclear and broad-component contributions; and assess line ratios sensitive to partially ionised gas where they are reliably measured. A width excess can motivate shocks, but does not identify them on its own. A sulfur ratio consistent with low density can constrain the emitting phase, but does not establish dilution as the cause of the BPT change.

### E5. What the model explains, and what it does not yet predict

The present model explains how a declining young-star contribution can produce lower total line luminosities, lower EW under a slowly changing continuum, and larger selected forbidden-line-to-Balmer ratios under a specified component mixture. These are useful, connected predictions. A spatially varying gas absorption term could link them to inner persistence and outer disappearance, but that extension is not yet computed against the observed selection.

It does not presently predict SF/NSF/ND fractions, fitted BPT classifications, electron densities, or line widths. Predicting those quantities would require spatial distributions, gas structure, radiation or shock calculations, an instrumental response, and the actual detection and classification rules. It also does not measure a stripping timescale or demonstrate that the observed galaxies are successive versions of one initial system. Calling it a physical interpretation or an illustrative model is accurate; calling it a validated evolutionary reconstruction would exceed the evidence.

The source note should be revised before any of its text or equations are transferred to a manuscript. Its Armitage citation has an inconsistent year and points to accretion-disc material that is not the appropriate foundation for the galaxy gas regulator. The galaxy-regulator references above provide the relevant context. There are also duplicated derivative/subscript notation issues in its later equations. No changes were made to that note in this task; the equations presented here use consistent reservoir notation and state the missing absorption assumption explicitly.
